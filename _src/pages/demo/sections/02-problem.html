The Problem

The music in most stores was never designed to support purchases.

Alcott sells elevated everyday objects. Ceramics, textiles, small furniture. Their customers don't rush. They browse with intent. They pick things up, put them down, pick them up again.

What music should be playing for them?

Right now, most stores like this have three options: a poorly curated playlist, the associate's personal Spotify (which risks licensing violations because consumer accounts aren't licensed for commercial use), or outsourcing the vibe to a big-box commercial music provider. In all three cases, the content of the songs was never designed to support customer behavior. It just has to be inviting enough not to drive people out. That's the bar.

But music does more than "not drive people out." Tempo affects walking pace. Major and minor keys affect mood differently. Instrumentation carries cultural meaning. Lyrical content suggests things. And familiarity cuts both ways: when a customer hears a song she knows and likes, research shows she perceives time as passing faster. She feels like she's been in the store longer. She wants to leave sooner. Even the songs that seem like wins are working against the sale.

None of these factors are deliberate in a typical retail music setup. They're incidental. Every song is a roll of the dice across dozens of variables that affect whether a customer stays, browses, and buys.

Entuned makes all of it intentional.
