The Hidden Cost of Your Licensing Fee

Your music line item is small. The costs sitting behind it are not.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-03-01 · 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • The monthly music subscription is usually the smallest piece of what a chain actually pays for in-store audio
  • ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR each collect separately, and most operators discover this only after a letter arrives
  • The identical-store problem means your playlist is running in other chains in your category for the same fee

A Director of Operations at a 28-store specialty chain opened her vendor spend report last quarter and asked her controller a simple question. Why is the music line so hard to pin down.

The answer took a week. The monthly subscription was $68 a store. Support and hardware maintenance was another line item. A performance rights invoice from ASCAP had landed in accounts payable in January. A second one from BMI had landed in March. SESAC had sent something in February that got filed under compliance. Nobody at corporate had a single number for what in-store music actually cost the business.

This is normal. Most multi-location operators we talk to have four or five touchpoints for a single category of spend, and none of the touchpoints reconcile.

What the subscription does and doesn't cover #

The monthly fee to a Mood Media, a Soundtrack, a Cloud Cover, a Rockbot, is a license to stream from their curated library. That fee usually includes the public performance rights that the vendor has negotiated on your behalf with the PROs. That part is real and worth paying for.

What the fee does not cover shows up in four places. Hardware and installation, which runs as a separate capital line and a separate maintenance contract. Content updates and support, which some vendors bundle and some charge for as an add-on. Occasional special-event licensing for in-store activations with live or streamed content. And the cost of the time your store managers spend reporting that the music stopped, the channel changed, or the volume was wrong when the district manager walked in.

The last one is the category nobody invoices but everybody absorbs.

ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and now GMR #

Most operators assume the music vendor handles performance rights. Most vendors do, for the catalog they provide. The trap is the edge cases. A store that plays a Spotify playlist off a staff phone during setup. A flagship that streams a live DJ on opening night. A holiday event with a local acoustic act. Any of those can generate a PRO letter, and the letters do not land in the same inbox as the monthly music invoice.

There are four performance rights organizations collecting in the United States now. ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, and GMR. Each licenses a different slice of the repertoire. Each can send an operator a separate demand. The blanket license you signed with your music vendor probably covers three of them for the catalog they stream. It does not cover anything your staff played off a personal device, and it does not cover live performance.

Most operators learn this the way the Director of Operations above learned it. In the middle of a spend audit, with a controller asking why there are four invoices for a thing they thought was one invoice.

The identical-store problem #

The other cost nobody prices is sameness. A catalog subscription ships you a playlist that serves a profile: upscale contemporary, quiet cafe, street boutique, whatever the vendor calls it. If a competing chain in your category picked the same profile, both chains sound the same to a customer who walks into both in the same afternoon.

Operators spend real money on visual differentiation. The fixtures, the lighting, the signage, the window. The audio plays for every hour a customer is inside and gets sourced from the same pool any other retailer can access for the same fee. The differentiation budget and the music budget are pulling in opposite directions and the monthly music line does not show this cost.

What a CFO can do this quarter #

Ask the controller to pull every invoice with “music,” “audio,” “performance rights,” “ASCAP,” “BMI,” “SESAC,” or “GMR” in the vendor name or memo line, for the trailing twelve months. Reconcile to a single per-store, per-month number. Most chains find the real number is two to four times the subscription line on its own.

Then ask your current music vendor three questions. What does our blanket license cover and not cover. How many other chains in our category are running our profile right now. If we wanted music that was not running in any other store, what would that cost.

Most vendors can answer the first question on the call. The second and the third usually take a week, and sometimes the answer is that the vendor cannot do it.
