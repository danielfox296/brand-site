How to Make Your Store Sound Premium

Premium audio is a set of parameters, not a genre. Tempo under 85 BPM, acoustic instrumentation, and clean production.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-04-01 · 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Premium audio is defined by specific parameters: tempo under 85 BPM, acoustic instrumentation, clean production, volume below 60 dB, unfamiliar tracks
  • Areni and Kim documented a 2.5x increase in average wine purchase price when classical music replaced pop
  • The 8-12% increase in willingness to pay works through associative memory, not genre labels
  • You do not need a luxury brand to benefit from premium priming

There is a version of “premium” that most stores get wrong. They play jazz because jazz seems sophisticated. Or they play classical because classical seems expensive. The genre is a proxy for something more specific, and the proxy is unreliable.

Jazz at 65 BPM with a piano trio creates a premium atmosphere. Jazz at 160 BPM with a bebop quintet creates anxiety. Both are jazz. They produce opposite effects. Classical with a string quartet creates elegance. Classical with a full orchestra at forte creates intensity. The genre label does not predict the outcome. The specific musical characteristics do.

What premium sounds like at the parameter level #

Premium audio is a set of production and compositional characteristics, not a genre. Understanding them at the parameter level is more useful than relying on labels.

Tempo: 60 to 85 BPM. Slower tempos create a sense of unhurried spaciousness. Milliman’s research shows slower music increases dwell time. In a premium context, the unhurried pace also signals that the store values the customer’s time and does not need to rush them through.

Instrumentation: acoustic and organic. Piano, guitar, strings, woodwinds, brushed drums, upright bass. These timbres carry associative weight from decades of presence in premium contexts. Concert halls, fine restaurants, luxury advertising, boutique hotels. The customer does not analyze the instrumentation consciously. The associations transfer automatically.

Production: clean and dynamic. Minimal compression. Wide dynamic range. Natural room sound. Heavily compressed music (where all frequencies are pushed to maximum volume) signals radio, mass-market retail, and high-energy commercial environments. Clean production signals care, space, and quality.

Volume: below 60 dB. Premium environments are quiet. The music fills the space without competing with conversation. A customer evaluating a $400 purchase needs cognitive space for that decision. Loud music competes for that space.

Familiarity: low. Unfamiliar music extends perceived dwell time (Yalch and Spangenberg, 2000). In a premium environment where longer browsing converts to higher transaction values, unfamiliar tracks are a structural advantage.

Vocals: minimal or absent. Lyrics demand cognitive attention. In a store where the goal is product evaluation and purchase confidence, instrumental music or music with atmospheric, non-narrative vocals keeps the customer’s attention on the merchandise.

The priming mechanism #

Luxury priming works through associative memory. The customer has decades of experience associating certain sounds with quality contexts. When they hear those sounds in your store, their brain activates the quality association automatically. Price expectations shift upward. Product evaluations shift toward favorable. The store “feels” right.

Areni and Kim documented this in 1993 when classical music in a wine store increased average purchase price 2.5x. The customers were not wine experts responding to the music analytically. They were ordinary shoppers whose ambient quality perception was being shifted by the audio environment.

The 8 to 12 percent increase in willingness to pay that research documents when music matches customer identity is largely this mechanism at work. The music confirms the customer’s self-concept as someone who shops in premium environments. That confirmation lowers price resistance. For more on how this works across price points, see luxury priming is real and you don’t have to be a luxury brand to use it.

What undermines premium audio #

Playing familiar pop hits on a “chill” channel. Recognizable songs trigger time-perception distortion and activate mass-market associations, even if the tempo is slow.

Volume set by employees during morning setup. An empty store sounds quieter than a full one. Music set at a comfortable volume at 9am is too loud at 2pm when the store has customers.

Compressed, radio-style production. Even acoustic genres can be produced with heavy compression for radio play. A compressed acoustic guitar track signals “coffee shop playlist,” not premium retail.

Lyrics that compete with the purchase decision. A customer trying on a $300 jacket does not need her attention split between the fit in the mirror and the narrative playing through the speakers.

Inconsistency across dayparts. Premium at 10am and energetic pop at 4pm creates an identity crisis. The customer who walks in during both windows encounters a different brand each time.

Applied premium audio for non-luxury brands #

You do not need to sell $5,000 handbags to benefit from premium priming. Any brand that positions itself above commodity benefits from audio that reinforces that positioning. A $200 dress. A $45 candle. A $75 skincare set. These are all purchases where quality perception affects willingness to pay.

The parameters are the same regardless of price point. Slower tempo. Acoustic instrumentation. Clean production. Lower volume. Unfamiliar tracks. Minimal vocals. The degree of refinement can scale with the brand’s positioning, but the direction is consistent.
