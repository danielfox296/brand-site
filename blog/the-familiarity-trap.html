The Familiarity Trap

Your customers recognize the playlist. And they leave sooner because of it.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-04-01 · 4 min read
Key takeaways
  • Shoppers exposed to familiar music left the store sooner, even though they felt like they'd stayed longer
  • Unfamiliar music removed cognitive anchors and extended actual floor time
  • Most operators have never acted on this research because sourcing unfamiliar music at scale was impractical until recently

Walk into one of your stores on a Tuesday afternoon and listen. If you recognize the song within five seconds, so does your customer. And they’re already losing track of how long they’ve been there, in the wrong direction.

There’s a study from 2000 that landed in the Journal of Business Research and then mostly sat there. Richard Yalch and Eric Spangenberg ran shoppers through a retail environment with two conditions: familiar background music and unfamiliar background music. They tracked how long people actually stayed and how long those people believed they’d stayed. The results went in opposite directions.

Shoppers who heard music they recognized reported feeling like they’d been in the store for a while. Long enough. Time to go. But their actual visit was shorter. The group hearing unfamiliar music reported the opposite: they felt like they hadn’t been there very long. And the clock confirmed they’d stayed longer.

The mechanism is straightforward. When your customer hears a song they know, their brain picks it up. They’re filling in the next lyric, anticipating the chorus, placing the song in some memory. All of that cognitive engagement registers as time passing. The store hasn’t changed. The customer just convinced themselves they’ve given it enough time, and they head for the door.

Unfamiliar music doesn’t trigger that chain. Nothing to anticipate, nothing to place. The music recedes into the environment the way background music is supposed to. Time feels shorter. The customer stays.

Why Operators Keep Programming Familiar Music #

If this research has been sitting in a journal for a quarter century, why does every second retail location still play music that half their customers can sing along to?

The practical answer: sourcing unfamiliar music that still sounds right in a commercial environment was genuinely hard. Music that most people haven’t heard tends not to have gone through normal commercial distribution. Plenty of excellent tracks exist outside the mainstream, but finding them, clearing them, and building a rotation that holds up across dayparts and demographics was a full-time curation job. Most operators had other things to do.

So the default held. Familiar catalogs. Recognizable artists. Playlists that staff could tolerate and customers could hum along with. The Yalch and Spangenberg paper got cited in a few more academic articles, and customers kept leaving earlier than they needed to.

What Changed #

Original music has no prior distribution

The thing that kept operators stuck on familiar catalogs was supply. Unfamiliar music that also held up in a retail environment didn’t exist at scale.

That changed. Music generation tools have crossed a threshold in the last two years where newly created tracks are indistinguishable from commercially produced music to a casual listener. The production quality holds. The harmonic structure holds. And because the track has never existed before, never been distributed, never charted, never played on any platform anywhere, no customer walks in with a cognitive hook already attached.

No recognition. No lyric anticipation. No memory surfacing. The music sits behind the shopping experience instead of competing with it.

What You Can Do This Week #

Before you change anything about your music program, walk three of your stores during a slow weekday window and just listen. Pay attention to whether you recognize the tracks playing. Ask one staff member if they’ve heard the current song before. Ask yourself how many of your customers have heard it too.

That’s the starting point. One question: is the music in my stores familiar enough to trigger the exit impulse that Yalch and Spangenberg documented twenty-five years ago?

If the answer is probably yes, you’re not alone. Most multi-location retailers are running the same familiar catalog and absorbing the same cost.
