The Audio Gap in Retail Customer Experience

Retailers invest millions in visual merchandising and lighting. Audio gets a $35/month subscription and no measurement.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-04-01 · 4 min read
Key takeaways
  • A mid-range specialty retailer spends $375K-$750K on buildout and $200-$960/year on audio
  • Audio lacks the organizational infrastructure, visible accountability, and measurement that visual merchandising has
  • Congruent music and scent combinations are multiplicative, not additive -- getting audio right amplifies every other investment

Walk into any well-run specialty store and count the deliberate decisions. The floor material. The fixture finish. The lighting temperature. The ceiling height. The rack spacing. The mannequin positioning. The paint color on the accent wall. Somebody chose all of that. Somebody tested some of it. Somebody signed off on a budget for it.

Now notice the music. In most stores, it is the only element in the room that was not designed for the people in the room.

The Investment Gap #

Per square foot buildout cost for a mid-range specialty retailer

A mid-range specialty retailer opening a new location typically spends $150 to $300 per square foot on buildout. For a 2,500-square-foot store, that is $375,000 to $750,000. Lighting alone can run $15,000 to $40,000 depending on the design. Visual merchandising fixtures and displays run $20,000 to $60,000. Scent systems, where installed, cost $2,000 to $5,000 per year.

The audio subscription: $200 to $960 per year. Less than the cost of a single mannequin. Less than the monthly electricity bill. Often less than the cleaning service.

The investment gap is not proportional to the influence gap. Atmosphere includes everything the customer senses. Audio is one of the most persistent of those inputs. The customer can look away from a display. They cannot turn off their ears.

Why Audio Gets Treated Differently #

Three structural reasons explain the gap.

First, visual merchandising has a mature industry around it. Dedicated roles (visual merchandising director, brand experience lead), dedicated budgets, dedicated measurement. Audio has none of this organizational infrastructure. Nobody in the typical retail org chart is accountable for what comes out of the speakers.

Second, visual changes are visible to the decision maker. A VP of retail can walk a store and see whether the displays are right. They cannot walk a store and hear whether the music is right because they do not know what “right” sounds like in measurable terms. They know what they personally like. That is not the same thing.

Third, audio lacks measurement. Visual merchandising teams can A/B test displays and measure sell-through by fixture. They can track which endcap drives more units. Audio has no equivalent measurement infrastructure in most stores. Without measurement, there is no data to justify investment. Without investment, there is no measurement. The cycle perpetuates itself.

What the Research Says About Relative Influence #

The academic literature on retail atmospherics treats audio as a primary environmental variable, not a secondary one. Turley and Milliman’s 2000 review of 60 atmospherics studies found that music was among the most consistently documented influences on consumer behavior. Effects on dwell time, pace of movement, product evaluation, spending, and mood were replicated across multiple decades and contexts.

Mattila and Wirtz (2001) demonstrated that congruent combinations of music and scent produced a significantly more positive customer experience than either element alone. The sensory elements are multiplicative, not additive. Getting the audio right amplifies the return on every other atmospheric investment.

What Closing the Gap Looks Like #

Closing the audio gap does not require the same budget as visual merchandising. It requires treating audio as a designed element rather than a default condition. That means choosing music based on customer psychology rather than staff preference, controlling the specific variables (tempo, instrumentation, familiarity, volume) that research has documented, and measuring the effect.

The measurement piece is where most stores stall. A $35 per month music subscription cannot tell you whether the music is helping. It can tell you what played. The gap between those two things is where the entire opportunity sits.
