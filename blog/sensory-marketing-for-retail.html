Sensory Marketing for Retail: Where Sound Fits in the Strategy

Daniel Fox · April 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR: Sensory marketing uses sight, sound, scent, touch, and taste to influence customer behavior. Scent and lighting get dedicated budgets and specialized vendors. Sound gets a $35 subscription and a mood channel. The research says congruent combinations of music and scent produce better outcomes than either alone. Sound is the most underinvested sensory channel in most retail environments.

Sensory marketing entered the mainstream retail vocabulary about a decade ago. The idea that stores should design for all five senses, not just sight, is no longer novel. Abercrombie made scent marketing famous. Apple made touch marketing a competitive advantage. Starbucks made the sound and smell of coffee preparation a brand asset.

But the way most retailers practice sensory marketing is lopsided. Visual is the dominant channel, which makes sense given that retail was built around visual merchandising. Scent has emerged as the second most developed channel, with companies like ScentAir and Prolitec serving thousands of locations. Touch is addressed through materials, textures, and interactive displays.

Sound is the gap. Not because retailers have decided it does not matter, but because the tools available to design with sound are blunt compared to what is available for every other sense.

The Five Channels and Their Investment Levels

Sight: Visual merchandising is a profession with dedicated budgets, roles, and measurement. Fixture design, lighting design, signage, color, and display are all specified, measured, and refined. Investment: $50,000 to $200,000+ per store in fixtures and displays alone.

Scent: Scent marketing has matured into a real category. ScentAir serves 80,000+ locations. Prolitec, AirQ, and AromaSys compete in the enterprise market. Retailers can specify scent notes, intensity, and scheduling. Annual cost: $2,000 to $6,000 per location. Small but deliberate.

Touch: Materials, fabrics, interactive displays, try-before-you-buy stations. Investment varies widely by category but is embedded in fixture and product display budgets.

Taste: Limited to food and beverage retail, hospitality, and occasional sampling programs.

Sound: A commercial music subscription. $200 to $960 per year. Genre selection. No targeting to customer psychology. No measurement of behavioral effect. No specification beyond 'play the chill channel.'

The gap between sound and the other channels is not proportional to their relative influence on customer behavior.

What the Research Says About Cross-Modal Effects

Mattila and Wirtz published a study in 2001 that tested the interaction between music and scent in a retail environment. Their finding: when the arousal levels of music and scent were congruent (both relaxing or both stimulating), customers reported significantly higher satisfaction and more positive evaluations of the store. When the sensory channels were mismatched, the effect worsened.

The implication is that sensory marketing is not additive. Installing a scent system and a music service independently does not produce the sum of their individual effects. The two need to be congruent. A relaxing lavender scent paired with high-energy dance music creates a sensory conflict that produces worse outcomes than either element alone.

Most retailers with scent programs do not coordinate them with their audio. The scent vendor and the music vendor have never spoken to each other. The two systems operate on separate schedules with separate design logics. The result is random congruence. Sometimes the scent and music align. Sometimes they conflict. Nobody is tracking which condition produces better sales days.

Why Sound Is the Hardest Channel to Get Right

Scent is easier to specify than sound. A retailer can say 'we want a citrus-forward scent with woody base notes' and the vendor can deliver exactly that. The scent does not change every three minutes.

Music changes constantly. A 10-hour operating day might include 150 to 200 individual tracks, each with different tempo, energy, instrumentation, key, and mood. The variability within a single day of music is orders of magnitude higher than the variability in the scent or lighting program. Controlling that variability requires controlling the musical parameters at the track level, not just the genre level.

Lighting designers specify in Kelvin and lumens. Scent designers specify in fragrance families and diffusion rates. Music gets 'indie chill.' The specification tools are not equivalent. Until they are, sound will remain the channel that retailers invest the least thought in, despite evidence that it is among the most influential.

Designing Sound Into the Sensory Stack

If you already have a scent program, align your audio to the same arousal level. Relaxing scent pairs with lower-tempo, acoustic music. Energizing scent pairs with moderate-tempo, rhythmic music. The congruence multiplies the effect of both.

If you are designing a store from scratch, specify the audio alongside the lighting and scent from the beginning. Define the target customer's psychological profile first, then derive the sensory parameters from that profile. What lighting temperature matches this customer? What scent? What musical characteristics?

The stores that do this well tend to be the ones whose brand experience is most cohesive. The customer cannot articulate why the store feels right. It feels right because every sensory channel is sending the same signal.
