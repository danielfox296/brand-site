What Music to Play in a High-End Store

Classical music increased average wine purchase price 2.5x in the Areni and Kim study. Premium audio is a set of production characteristics, not a genre.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-04-01 · 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Classical music increased average wine purchase price 2.5x in the Areni and Kim study — same units sold, higher value
  • Premium audio is a set of production characteristics: acoustic instrumentation, clean production, melodic complexity, restrained energy
  • Recommended parameters for high-end retail: tempo under 85 BPM, volume under 60 dB, low vocal content, unfamiliar tracks
  • Most commercial music services let you filter by genre but not by compression ratio, timbral warmth, or harmonic complexity

There is a specific kind of store that spends months choosing the right marble for the floor, the right temperature for the lighting, and the right weight for the shopping bags. Everything the customer sees and touches has been specified. Then somebody picks a “chill vibes” playlist and calls it done.

The audio environment in a high-end store is doing more work than most operators realize. It is either confirming the quality narrative that every other design element is building, or it is contradicting it. The research on this is not ambiguous.

The Areni and Kim Finding #

2.5x

Areni and Kim, 1993

In 1993, Areni and Kim published a study that remains one of the cleanest demonstrations of music’s effect on purchasing behavior. They alternated classical music and top-40 pop music in a wine store over a controlled period. Total units sold did not change. But average transaction value did.

When classical music played, customers selected more expensive bottles. The average purchase price was roughly 2.5 times higher than during the pop music condition. The customers did not buy more wine. They bought better wine.

The mechanism is quality inference. Classical music activated an associative network linked to sophistication, taste, and premium quality. That association transferred to the products in the environment. The customers were not thinking about the music. They were thinking about wine. The music was shaping how they thought about the wine.

How Quality Inference Transfers #

The Areni and Kim study was conducted in a wine store, but the mechanism is not wine-specific. Quality inference through music operates the same way across product categories. The customer’s brain builds an ambient context from every sensory input in the environment. Lighting, materials, scent, spacing, and sound all contribute to a composite impression of quality level.

When the audio signals premium, the customer’s internal price calibration shifts upward. A $300 jacket feels appropriately priced. A $45 candle feels reasonable. The products have not changed. The context in which the customer evaluates them has.

When the audio signals mass-market, the calibration shifts downward. The same $300 jacket now feels overpriced. The customer might not articulate it that way. They just feel less certain about the purchase. The browsing feels slightly off. They leave without buying, or they buy less than they would have.

What Premium Audio Actually Sounds Like #

Premium is not a genre. It is a set of production characteristics. Understanding what they are is more useful than picking a genre label.

Acoustic instrumentation. Piano, guitar, strings, woodwinds, and jazz ensembles carry stronger quality associations than synthesized or electronically produced sounds. This is a learned association built from decades of exposure to acoustic music in premium contexts: concert halls, fine dining, luxury advertising.

Clean production. Minimal compression, wide dynamic range, natural reverb. Heavily compressed pop music — where every frequency band is pushed to maximum loudness — signals radio and mass-market contexts. Clean production signals care, space, and refinement.

Melodic complexity. Simple, repetitive hooks signal pop accessibility. Melodic development, harmonic movement, and structural variation signal sophistication. The customer does not need to understand music theory to perceive this. The distinction is processed at an intuitive level.

Restrained energy. Premium audio tends to sit in a lower energy band. Tempos between 60 and 85 BPM. Dynamics that breathe rather than pump. This creates a sense of spaciousness that aligns with the physical spaciousness of premium retail environments.

Vocal restraint. Lyrics compete for cognitive attention. In a high-end store where the goal is contemplation and evaluation, instrumental music or music with minimal, non-distracting vocals reduces cognitive load. The customer’s attention stays on the product rather than following a lyrical narrative.

Common Mistakes in High-End Retail Audio #

The most common mistake is playing music the staff likes rather than music the customer needs. Store managers in premium environments tend to default to cool indie playlists because the music feels sophisticated to them. But “cool” and “premium” are different signals. Indie rock primes a different associative context than jazz trio or contemporary classical.

The second mistake is volume. High-end stores should be among the quietest retail environments. Music below 58 dB creates an intimate, unhurried atmosphere. Many stores run 10 to 15 dB louder than they should because the staff sets the volume during setup when the store is empty.

The third mistake is familiarity. Playing recognizable songs — even sophisticated ones — triggers the time-perception distortion documented by Yalch and Spangenberg. Customers who recognize the music perceive that they have been shopping longer than they have. In a premium environment where extended browsing leads to higher transaction values, that is the wrong direction.

What This Means Practically #

If you operate a high-end store and you want the audio environment to support your price position, the parameters are: tempo under 85 BPM, acoustic instrumentation, clean production, low vocal content, volume under 60 dB, and unfamiliar tracks. Those are not aesthetic opinions. They are variables with documented effects on willingness to pay and dwell time.

Most commercial music services let you filter by genre. None of them let you filter by compression ratio, timbral warmth, or harmonic complexity. The gap between what the research says matters and what the tools let you control is where most premium stores lose the thread.
