Retail Music Licensing in 2026: What Every Store Owner Needs to Know

ASCAP, BMI, SESAC: what licenses your store needs, what they cost, and how AI-generated music changes the equation.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-04-01 · 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Any music played in a retail store requires public performance licenses from ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC — consumer streaming services do not include them
  • Annual PRO costs range from $800 to $2,000 per location, or $200-$960 through a bundled commercial service
  • AI-generated music requires no performance licenses — eliminating $8,000-$20,000 annually for a 10-location chain

Music licensing for retail is not complicated once you understand the structure. But the structure is not intuitive, the terminology is overloaded, and the consequences of getting it wrong are financial.

This guide covers what licenses exist, who enforces them, what they cost, and how the emergence of AI-generated music changes the calculation.

The Three Organizations You Need to Know #

In the United States, public performance rights for music are administered by three performing rights organizations: ASCAP (American Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers), BMI (Broadcast Music, Inc.), and SESAC (originally the Society of European Stage Authors and Composers).

Each organization represents a different pool of songwriters and publishers. A song’s performance rights are held by one of the three. Since you cannot predict which songs your store will play, you typically need blanket licenses from all three to be fully covered.

A blanket license gives you the right to publicly perform any work in that organization’s catalog for a fixed annual fee. It is the standard approach for retail.

What Counts as a Public Performance #

Under U.S. copyright law (17 U.S.C. Section 106(4)), a public performance includes playing music in any place open to the public or where a substantial number of people outside your normal circle of family and friends can hear it. A retail store qualifies.

This applies regardless of the source. Streaming service, CD, vinyl, radio, USB drive, phone connected to a Bluetooth speaker. The medium does not matter. The act of playing copyrighted music in a commercial space is a public performance and requires authorization.

The one narrow exception: retail establishments under 2,000 square feet playing over-the-air radio or television broadcasts through six or fewer speakers. This exemption does not cover streaming, satellite radio, CDs, or any digital source.

ASCAP’s retail license is tiered by square footage. For a typical retail location (2,000 to 10,000 square feet), the annual fee ranges from approximately $400 to $900. BMI’s retail license is structured similarly, running $350 to $800 per year for comparable locations.

SESAC negotiates individually. Their catalog is smaller but includes significant commercial repertoire. Expect $200 to $500 per year for a single retail location.

Total annual PRO cost for one store: roughly $800 to $2,000. For a 10-location chain: $8,000 to $20,000. For a 50-location fleet: $40,000 to $100,000.

These costs are on top of whatever you pay for the music itself. A $20 per month commercial streaming service plus $1,500 per year in PRO fees brings the real annual cost to $1,740 per location, not $240.

How Enforcement Works #

ASCAP and BMI employ field investigators who visit commercial establishments to verify licensing status. If a business is playing music and does not have a license on file, the investigator documents the infringement and the organization sends a cease-and-desist letter.

Most businesses receive a letter first. The letter offers the option to purchase a license retroactively. If the business ignores the letter or refuses to comply, the PRO can file a copyright infringement lawsuit. Statutory damages under U.S. law range from $750 to $30,000 per work infringed. Willful infringement can reach $150,000 per work.

Enforcement has increased in recent years. Both ASCAP and BMI have expanded their compliance teams and use monitoring technology alongside field investigators. The probability of enforcement action correlates with location visibility.

The Commercial Music Service Shortcut #

The simplest way to handle licensing is to use a commercial music service that includes blanket PRO licenses in the subscription fee. Most reputable services do this. Pandora CloudCover, Soundtrack Your Brand, SiriusXM for Business, and Mood Media all include performance licensing in their subscription costs.

At $17 to $80 per month per location, the commercial service approach is almost always cheaper than securing PRO licenses independently and paying for a separate music source. For a single location, CloudCover at $204 per year (annual prepay) versus $800 to $2,000 per year in direct PRO fees plus whatever you pay for the music itself.

One caution: verify that your service explicitly states that all necessary public performance licenses are included. Some budget services handle licensing through the platform but do not cover all three PROs.

AI-Generated Music and the Licensing Question #

AI-generated original music introduces a fundamentally different licensing situation. When no human songwriter created the music, there are no performance rights to administer. No ASCAP membership. No BMI catalog entry. No SESAC affiliation.

The U.S. Copyright Office has consistently held that works generated by AI without substantial human authorship are not eligible for copyright registration. No copyright means no performance right to license. A retail store playing AI-generated music is not performing a copyrighted work and has no PRO licensing obligation.

Multiple jurisdictions have issued guidance in 2025 and 2026 supporting commercial use of AI-generated music, with the primary constraint being that the AI model’s training data was properly sourced. For retailers, the practical implication is that AI-generated music eliminates the entire PRO licensing layer.
