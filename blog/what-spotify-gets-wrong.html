Is It Actually Illegal to Play Spotify in Your Stores?

A store manager hits play on a personal Spotify account. A regional director gets a letter from ASCAP three months later. Here is the clean operator answer to what is actually allowed.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-03-01 · 4 min read
Key takeaways
  • A personal Spotify account playing on a store speaker is a terms of service violation and a public performance rights problem.
  • Spotify for Business and Soundtrack Your Brand cover the streaming side but you still need to think about what happens when a staff member logs in with their own phone.
  • ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC send letters to multi-location retailers more often than most operators realize.
  • The safest answer is music your company owns or licenses in a way that survives a staff turnover event.

A regional director at a 22 store accessories chain calls me last November. She got a letter from ASCAP. The letter referenced three of her stores by name. A staff member had been playing a personal Spotify account through the store speaker on Tuesday afternoons. ASCAP wanted a licensing fee for the previous 18 months.

She asked me the same question most operators eventually ask. Is it actually illegal to play Spotify in my stores, or is this a shakedown?

The answer is a little of both, and the fix is simpler than most vendors let on.

What Spotify's own terms say #

A personal Spotify subscription is for personal use. The terms of service are clear about this. Playing that account through a store speaker during operating hours violates the terms. Spotify can suspend the account. Most operators never get suspended. They get something worse later.

The separate issue is public performance rights. When music plays in a retail space, that counts as a public performance under US copyright law. Public performance needs a license from the performance rights organizations, ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC. A personal Spotify subscription does not include that license. The artists and publishers never got paid for the commercial use, which is what the PROs exist to collect on.

This is the part most store managers do not know. They assume a paid subscription covers everything. It does not.

How retailers usually learn this #

ASCAP and BMI field reps visit malls and shopping districts. They note what music is playing in which store. They cross reference the stores against their own database of licensed businesses. If a store is not licensed, they send a letter.

The letters are firm but negotiable. The going rate for a small chain is a few thousand dollars per year in back fees plus a forward license. Operators who ignore the letters get lawsuits. Operators who respond usually settle.

I have seen three versions of this over the past two years. In every case, the store manager had been playing a personal account because the corporate music service was broken, boring, or both. The licensing exposure was real. The underlying cause was that the approved music was not good enough to keep the staff from reaching for their own phone.

What actually covers you #

Spotify for Business and Soundtrack Your Brand include the public performance license as part of the subscription. So do Mood Media, Cloud Cover, Rockbot, and most B2B music providers. If you pay for one of these and you can demonstrate that every store is covered, you are legally clean on that side.

The part that still trips operators up is enforcement inside the store. A licensed B2B subscription does not stop a staff member from plugging their phone in when the sanctioned playlist gets repetitive. Now you have the licensed service running on paper and an unlicensed personal account running on the speaker. ASCAP does not care what you paid for. They care what is playing.

The operators who stay out of trouble do two things. They pay for a real commercial music service at every location. And they give their stores music that the staff does not want to replace.

The cost trap nobody mentions #

Commercial music subscriptions look cheap per store per month. The math stops working at scale. A 40 store chain paying for a B2B subscription plus the add ons most vendors push, messaging channels, drive thru audio, seasonal swaps, is writing a real check every year for something most operators never audit.

When was the last time your team evaluated the music line item against what the stores actually hear? When was the last time anyone in ops listened to a full shift in a flagship store and wrote down what played?

Those two questions usually produce a conversation the finance team has been waiting for.

What to do this week #

Three small moves before this becomes a letter. Walk two stores during a mid week afternoon shift and listen to what is actually playing. Ask your music vendor for proof of PRO coverage in writing, naming ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC. Check whether any of your stores have a personal Bluetooth speaker paired to a staff device.

If the answer to the third question is yes in even one location, you have the exposure this post describes.
