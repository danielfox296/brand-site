Multi-Location Music: The Brand Problem

Ten locations, ten managers, ten soundtracks. Your brand has a Pantone for every surface but no spec for audio.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-04-01 · 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Multi-location brands specify fonts, colors, and signage — but leave music to whoever is closest to the iPad
  • Mattila and Wirtz (2001) showed congruent sensory environments produce significantly better customer experiences
  • Genre-level centralized control ensures consistency but not effectiveness — consistent mediocrity is still mediocrity
  • A real audio spec uses parameters (tempo, instrumentation, volume) the way a lighting spec uses Kelvin and lumens

If you run more than one location, you have this problem even if you do not know it. Your brand guidelines specify fonts, colors, photography style, tone of voice, signage placement, and packaging materials. Your operations manual covers layout, stocking procedures, cleaning schedules, and customer greeting protocols.

The music is probably a subscription login and a note that says “play appropriate music.” What appropriate means is left to the person standing closest to the iPad.

How the drift happens #

Store managers are humans with personal taste. A manager in one location gravitates toward ambient electronic. Another plays R&B. A third uses whatever station was selected when the service was set up two years ago and has not changed it since. A fourth turns the music off during slow periods because they find it annoying.

None of this is malicious or even careless. It is the predictable result of deploying a creative element with no specification and no oversight. Every other brand element in the store has a spec. The music does not.

Over time, the drift compounds. Each store develops its own audio identity. That identity is shaped by whoever was last promoted to assistant manager, not by the brand team that specified everything else the customer encounters.

Why it matters #

The audio environment contributes to brand perception. Mattila and Wirtz’s 2001 research showed that congruent sensory environments produce significantly better customer experiences than mismatched ones. When the music in your Denver store feels premium and the music in your Austin store feels like a college dorm room, customers in those two stores are forming different impressions of the same brand.

For single-location operators, the music reflects whatever the owner or manager prefers. That is a choice, and it can be a good one. For multi-location brands, inconsistency is not a choice. It is a failure of specification.

This problem is invisible to the executive team because nobody reports on it. The regional manager walking a store inspection is checking signage compliance, fixture condition, and stocking levels. They are probably not listening critically to the audio and comparing it against a documented standard, because no documented standard exists. What centralized control looks like #

Most commercial music services offer some form of centralized management. Soundtrack Your Brand, SiriusXM for Business, and Mood Media all provide dashboards where a corporate user can set playlists, schedules, and station selections across locations. The store manager sees the player. The brand team controls the programming.

This solves the consistency problem at the genre level. Every store plays the same station. Every location follows the same daypart schedule. The brand team picks the mood, the service delivers it uniformly.

What centralized playlist management does not solve: whether the music is right for the customer. It ensures every store plays the same thing. It does not ensure the thing being played is effective. Consistent mediocrity is still mediocrity.

The specification problem #

A lighting designer specifies color temperature in Kelvin, brightness in lumens, beam angle in degrees. These are measurable parameters. Any electrician in any city can execute the spec and produce the same result.

Music has equivalent parameters. Tempo in BPM. Volume in decibels. Key. Mode. Instrumentation profile. Timbral warmth. Production style. The difference is that no commercial music service lets you specify at this level. You get genre labels and mood descriptors. You cannot say “every location plays 70 to 85 BPM, acoustic instrumentation, clean production, minor-to-mixed mode, below 60 dB.” You can only say “play the jazz channel.”

The jazz channel in one daypart might be 65 BPM piano trio. In another it might be 140 BPM bebop. Both are jazz. They produce opposite effects on customer behavior. The genre label is too coarse to be a specification.

What would solve it #

A real audio specification for a multi-location brand would look like a lighting spec. Target tempo range. Instrumentation parameters. Production characteristics. Volume calibration per location (accounting for room size and ambient noise). Familiarity constraints. And measurement at each location to confirm the spec is producing the intended effect.

This requires music that is parameterized, not just categorized. If every track has logged values for tempo, key, instrumentation, energy, and production style, the brand team can define ranges for each parameter and ensure compliance across the fleet. Deviation becomes measurable. The equivalent of a store using the wrong font becomes a store playing music outside the specified parameters.
