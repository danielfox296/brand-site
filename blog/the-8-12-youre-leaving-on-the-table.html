The Margin You're Leaving on the Table

When in-store music matches who your customer is, willingness to pay rises. Same products, same prices. Most stores leave that margin on the table.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-03-01 · 4 min read
Key takeaways
  • Music congruent with customer identity raises willingness to pay, shown across lab and field studies of music and retail (North, Sheridan and Areni, 2016; Flynn et al., 2022)
  • The effect requires no additional foot traffic, no promotions, and no pricing changes
  • Most in-store music vendors were built to fill silence, not to match your customer

You spent six figures on the buildout. You negotiated the lease. You hired visual merchandisers to make sure the product looks right from the door. And then someone plugged in a Mood Media box and hit play on a playlist that could belong to literally any store in America.

There is a finding buried in the retail psychology literature that most operators have never seen. It runs through decades of studies on how the music in a space shapes what shoppers will pay, and it has been replicated enough times that arguing with it feels like arguing with gravity.

Higher
Willingness to pay when in-store music matches the customer's identity, versus generic music
North, Sheridan & Areni, 2016; Flynn et al., 2022

When the music in a retail space matches the identity and values of the customers shopping there, willingness to pay goes up. Same products on the shelves. Same prices on the tags. Same staff on the floor. Customers just pay more.

Areni and Kim published the foundational work in 1993, showing that the music in a wine store shifted which bottles customers chose. Later research isolated the willingness-to-pay effect directly: North, Sheridan, and Areni (2016) and Flynn and colleagues (2022) found that music congruent with a product’s identity raises how much customers are prepared to pay, and that the lift is specific to identity-laden products rather than purely functional ones. At this point, “does music affect willingness to pay” is a settled question. What remains open is which music, for which customer, and by how much.

How much revenue is bad music actually leaving on the table? #

Most retail music providers are catalog businesses. They organize licensed tracks into moods and genres and let operators pick from a menu. The assumption built into that model is that “upbeat” or “relaxed” or “contemporary” is a meaningful enough description of what a store needs.

Your customer is not “upbeat.” She is a 34-year-old woman who grew up on Lauryn Hill and shops at your store because it feels like it was made for someone like her. The music that makes her stay longer and spend more is specific to her, and it is different from the music that does the same for someone else in a different store three miles away.

Catalog providers were built to fill silence. They do that well. They were not built to match music to a customer’s identity at the level the research describes.

What a Few Points of Willingness to Pay Looks Like on Your P&L #

$326,400
Illustrative annual lift from an 8% bump on $85 average ticket across 4,000 monthly transactions — a worked example, not a research figure. Run it on your own numbers.
Worked example for mid-range specialty retail

Run the math on your own operation. Take your average transaction value. Apply a modest lift. Multiply by the number of transactions in a month across your locations. That is the margin sitting in the research, waiting for someone to go collect it.

It does not require more foot traffic. It does not require a promotion. It does not require a new product line. It requires that the person already standing in your store hears something that tells them this place was built for someone like them.

The stores playing generic playlists are giving that number back every day. Not because the music is bad, necessarily. Because the music is anonymous.

What You Can Do This Week #

Walk your highest-performing store and your lowest-performing store on the same day. Stand near the entrance for ten minutes in each one. Listen to what is playing. Ask yourself whether the music sounds like it belongs to your brand and your customer, or whether it sounds like it belongs to a dentist’s office, a hotel lobby, or nowhere in particular.

Then call your music vendor and ask them one question: what data are you using to select the tracks in my stores? If the answer is “genre” and “mood,” you are paying for silence-filling. That margin is still on the table.
