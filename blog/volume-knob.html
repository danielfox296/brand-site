Your Volume Knob Is a Free Lever

Volume is the simplest variable in the music-and-retail equation. Getting it right costs nothing. Getting it wrong costs dwell time.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-03-01 · 4 min read
Key takeaways
  • Louder music means higher arousal, faster perceived tempo, and shorter visits. The research on this goes back decades.
  • Most stores set volume when the floor is empty, then enter a loudness spiral once customers arrive
  • The free test: can two people converse at normal speaking level anywhere in the store? If not, the music is too loud.
  • Volume should change throughout the day. Almost nobody does this on purpose.

Your store manager opens at 9 AM. The floor is empty, the acoustics are dry, and the music sounds fine at the level she picked last month. By noon the store has forty people in it, three conversations happening at once, and the same volume setting now feels like it belongs in a different room.

So someone reaches for the knob and turns it up. That instinct is wrong, and it is costing you dwell time.

The Loudness Spiral #

When volume rises to compete with crowd noise, the store enters a loudness spiral. The music gets louder. Conversations get louder to compensate. The music goes up again. Within twenty minutes the environment is measurably more stressful than it needs to be.

Customers in loud environments make faster decisions, spend less time per item, and report lower satisfaction in post-visit surveys. They do not identify the volume as the cause. They just say the visit felt rushed, or that they did not find what they were looking for. The store manager blames the product assortment or the weather. The real problem was a dial set three notches too high.

This is well-documented. Ronald Milliman showed in the early 1980s that the pace and intensity of in-store music changed how fast customers moved through a grocery store and how much they spent. The research has only gotten more specific since then. Volume is one of the clearest levers in the entire body of literature on retail environments.

When Quiet Is the Stronger Brand Signal #

Walk into a store where the music sits at a moderate-to-low level and you feel something specific. The space is confident. Nobody is competing for your attention. That is the same signal expensive restaurants send with hushed dining rooms and luxury hotels send with muted lobbies.

A boutique playing music at the same volume as a mall food court is sending a message that contradicts everything the visual merchandising team spent six months building. The store director approved the fixtures, the lighting plan, the scent diffuser. Nobody approved the decibel level. Staff just kept nudging the knob up because it felt too quiet during a slow Tuesday.

Why Staff Keep Touching the Volume #

The staff volume problem is real. Walk any multi-location chain and you will find stores where the music is off entirely because an associate turned it down, then down again, then off. You will find other stores where the volume is cranked because the opening team likes it loud. Neither decision has anything to do with customers.

A policy memo will not solve this. Nobody reads those. Give your team a concrete standard they can check themselves. “Can two people have a conversation at normal speaking level anywhere on the floor?” That is the test. If customers have to raise their voices, the music is too loud. If the music disappears under conversation, it is too quiet.

Print the test on a card and put it behind the register. Make it part of the opening checklist. You will cut half your volume complaints in a month.

Set It Differently at Different Times #

Morning, when the store is empty and the ceiling bounces every note, needs a lower setting than Saturday afternoon when sixty people absorb the sound. This is obvious once someone says it out loud. Almost nobody adjusts for it deliberately.

Walk your stores at three different times this week. Stand in the middle of the floor and listen. Ask yourself whether the volume matches what the customer is supposed to feel at that moment. If it does not, adjust it. Then write down the levels so your opening and closing teams know what to set.

Volume is the one thing you can fix today without a vendor, a budget, or a meeting. Most of the audio environment is harder to get right. The music itself, the pacing, the way the sound shifts across a twelve-hour operating day, all of that takes real measurement and real strategy. But the knob on the wall is yours right now.
