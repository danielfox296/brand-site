What Your Store Says in the First 10 Seconds

Every visual choice in a well-run store is on purpose. The music is the only thing that got picked by whoever opened this morning.

Daniel Fox Founder & CEO · 2026-04-01 · 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • A customer reads your store in about ten seconds. Lighting, fixtures, staff posture, smell, sound. All of it arrives at once.
  • Every visual element in a well-run store was chosen by someone. The music was chosen by whoever opened this morning.
  • That mismatch is something customers feel without being able to name it.
  • Walking your own stores with fresh ears is the cheapest audit you can run this quarter.

Walk into one of your best locations on a random Tuesday at 11 AM. Stand just inside the door. Count to ten.

In that ten seconds, a customer has already decided a lot. Whether the place feels busy or dead. Whether the staff noticed them. Whether the lighting is flattering or fluorescent. Whether the smell is intentional or whatever the HVAC is doing that week. And whether the music belongs to this store, or belongs to whoever was at the POS this morning when they hit shuffle.

Every Other Signal Got a Meeting #

Think about how the rest of that ten seconds gets made.

The fixtures came out of a store planning session. Someone argued about the height of the tables and whether the front window should face the mannequin or the product wall. The lighting came out of a spec. Somebody picked the color temperature and the angle and signed a PO for the bulbs. The staff got hired, trained, handed a playbook on greeting, and coached on how to stand near the door without hovering. The sightline from the entrance to the back wall was walked a dozen times during the fit-out.

All of that is a person doing a job. Somebody owned each of those decisions and can tell you why the store looks and feels the way it does.

Now ask the same question about the music. Who picked it. When. Based on what. Most operators I talk to cannot answer. The honest answer is usually that the chain signed up for a music service in 2019, a regional manager picked a couple of stations once, and the 24-year-old running register at 9:15 this morning changed the channel because the first song annoyed him.

Why Customers Feel It Anyway #

The issue is not that customers walk in thinking about the music. They do not. The issue is that every other piece of the experience was built to line up, and the one piece that did not line up makes the whole thing feel slightly off.

Customers do not say “the music is wrong.” They say the store feels tired. They say it feels like a mall store even though you are on a nice street. They say it feels like the staff does not care, even when the staff is trying hard. They leave faster than you expected, or they linger but do not buy, and nobody can point at what broke the spell.

This is not a new finding. Retail researchers have been publishing on music and shopper behavior since Ronald Milliman’s grocery store work in the early 80s. The research is consistent. The sound in a room changes how long people stay and what they feel about the place while they are there. The finding has been sitting there for forty years waiting for somebody to take it seriously as an operational decision instead of a vibe.

The Reason It Got Skipped #

The reason music has lived in a different category than fixtures and lighting is practical. Nobody had the budget to hire a composer for a clothing store. Licensing a catalog and piping it into 40 locations was the only option that penciled, and a catalog by definition was made by artists for fans of those artists. The fit to any particular store was always approximate. Everyone in retail knew this. Nobody had a better move.

So in-store music got handled by a service running in the background and a playlist that nobody has reviewed since a district manager last visited. The staff stopped noticing it around week three. The customer walks in new every single time.

That gap is closing now, and operators who take it seriously in 2026 are going to get a year or two of quiet advantage before the rest of the market notices.

What to Do This Week #

You can audit this without us and without spending anything. Pick three stores. Drive to them on a weekday during a real shopping hour, not before open. Stand inside the door for thirty seconds before anyone recognizes you.

Write down three things. What is playing. Whether it sounds like the store you spent two years building, or whether it sounds like a random station. And whether it would still be playing if the staff thought a senior person was about to walk in.

If the answer to the third one is no, you already know you have a problem. You just have not had a reason to do anything about it yet.
