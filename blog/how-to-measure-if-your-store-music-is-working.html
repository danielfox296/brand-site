How to Measure If Your Store Music Is Working

Daniel Fox · April 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR: Most retailers cannot answer whether their music helps or hurts sales because their music provider gives them no behavioral data. Four metrics reveal whether your audio environment is producing measurable value: dwell time, conversion rate, average transaction value, and return visit frequency. If you cannot correlate changes in your music to changes in these metrics, you are paying for audio with no feedback on whether it works.

Ask any retailer what their conversion rate is and they will give you a number. Ask them what their average transaction value was last month and they have it. Ask whether the music in their store is increasing or decreasing those numbers and the conversation stops.

The problem is not that the answer is complicated. The problem is that no data exists to answer the question. The music provider tracks what was scheduled to play. The POS system tracks what was sold. Nobody connects the two.

Why Your Music Provider Cannot Tell You

Commercial music services are built to deliver and schedule music. Their reporting tells you which songs played at which times. Schedule adherence. Play counts. Station selections. This is operational data. It confirms the service is running.

What it does not tell you: did dwell time change when the playlist changed? Did conversion move when the tempo shifted? Did average transaction value respond to the genre rotation? Did the Thursday evening jazz experiment produce different results than the Friday morning pop selection?

This is not a feature gap. It is an architecture gap. Connecting audio variables to business outcomes requires logging the specific musical parameters of every track (not just the song name, but the tempo, key, instrumentation, energy, and genre blend), and then correlating those parameters against concurrent sales data, foot traffic, and dwell time.

No catalog-based music service does this because no catalog-based music service controls the musical parameters at that level. They select songs. The songs have whatever characteristics the original artist gave them. There is no parameterized input to correlate against.

The Four Metrics That Matter

If you want to know whether your music is working, these are the four metrics to watch. Each one has a documented relationship to audio environment variables in the research literature.

Dwell Time

How long customers stay in the store. The most consistently documented effect of music on retail behavior. Milliman's 1982 study showed slow tempo increased dwell time by 34%. Dozens of replications since then confirm the direction, though the magnitude varies by context.

Measurement requires foot traffic counting at entry and exit. If you have a RetailNext, Sensormatic, or similar system, you probably already have this data. The gap is that nobody is correlating it against what was playing when the dwell time moved.

Conversion Rate

The percentage of visitors who make a purchase. More sensitive to overall store execution than to any single variable, but audio environment contributes. Research shows that congruent music (music that matches customer psychology) increases purchase confidence. Incongruent music disrupts it.

If your conversion rate moves when you change the music and returns when you change it back, the music is a factor. If it does not move, the music may be neutral or the effect may be masked by stronger variables.

Average Transaction Value

What each customer spends per purchase. The metric most directly affected by quality inference and willingness-to-pay priming. Areni and Kim showed a 2.5x increase in average wine purchase price when classical music replaced pop. The mechanism is ambient priming of quality perception.

This is the metric where premium audio has the clearest measurable impact. If your music is priming quality perception, average transaction value should respond. If you switch from a generic mood channel to premium-positioned audio and ATV does not move, either the music is not different enough or the product mix is not responsive to priming.

Return Visit Frequency

How often customers come back. The hardest to attribute to any single variable but the most valuable over time. Mood Media's own research found that 90% of shoppers say they are more likely to revisit a store with enjoyable atmosphere. Audio is a major component of atmosphere.

Return visits compound. A 5% increase in return visit frequency over a year produces a meaningful change in lifetime customer value. The challenge is attribution. You need a loyalty system or customer identification method to track return visits, and you need enough time to see the pattern emerge.

What a Measurement Architecture Looks Like

Measuring music's effect on retail requires three components. First, parameterized music. Every track needs to be logged with its specific musical characteristics, not just a song name and artist. Tempo, key, mode, instrumentation, energy level, genre classification.

Second, concurrent business data. Sales by hour, foot traffic, dwell time, conversion rate. Most retailers already have this from their POS and analytics systems.

Third, a correlation engine that connects the two. When the music shifted from 72 BPM acoustic guitar at 2pm to 95 BPM electronic at 3pm, what happened to dwell time and ATV in the same window? Over enough iterations, patterns emerge. Specific musical parameters produce specific behavioral responses in your specific store with your specific customers.

This is not possible with a catalog-based music service that rotates songs without logging or controlling their parameters. It requires music where every variable is deliberate, logged, and adjustable.

Starting Without the Full Architecture

If you do not have a measurement system, you can still run crude tests. Pick a specific week. Note the music configuration (station, genre, approximate tempo). Record daily sales, traffic, and ATV from your POS. The next week, change one variable. Compare.

This is not statistically rigorous. A week of data in a single store is too noisy and too short to draw firm conclusions. But it builds the muscle of treating music as a variable rather than a fixed condition. And it surfaces the question: if I had better data, would I make different music decisions?
