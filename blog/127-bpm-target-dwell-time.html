I Was in a Target at 127 BPM. Here’s What That Costs a Retailer.

A quarter note at 127 is a foot-traffic accelerant. Most stores are running one and don’t know it.

Daniel Fox · May 2026 · 5 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Background music tempo directly affects how fast customers move through a store
  • 127 BPM is dance-floor speed. Most retail environments should sit between 72 and 96.
  • Slower tempos have been shown to increase sales by as much as 38%
  • Most retailers have no spec for the music playing in their stores

I was in a Target yesterday. Protein shakes and cat enzyme carpet cleaner. Not a research trip. But I’m a music producer who builds audio for retail environments, so I hear things in stores that most people feel without noticing.

There was a song playing over the PA. Electronica-leaning pop, catchy, well-produced. The kind of track someone at corporate probably picked because it sounded current. The store was mostly empty, mid-afternoon on a weekday, and this song was pushing 127 beats per minute.

I know this because I pulled out my phone and tapped along with a tempo-tracking app. A quarter note equaling 127 puts you squarely in dance music territory. The tempo of a DJ set at 11 p.m., not a retail floor at 2 p.m. on a Tuesday.

127 BPM. The tempo playing in a mostly-empty Target, mid-afternoon.

Why tempo matters more than you think

Your body syncs to external rhythmic stimuli. This is called entrainment, a physiological response documented across decades of neuroscience and music cognition research. Your heart rate, your gait, your breathing pattern, your perception of elapsed time. All of it responds to rhythmic input, including the background music playing overhead in a store.

When the tempo is fast, people walk faster. They browse less. They make quicker purchase decisions or skip purchases entirely. They leave sooner. A 127 BPM track costs you dwell time every second it plays.

Ronald Milliman published the foundational study on this in 1982. He tested slow versus fast background music in grocery stores and found that slow tempo music increased sales by 38.2% compared to fast tempo music. The customers didn’t buy different things. They stayed longer. They lingered. They covered more of the store. Dwell time went up, and basket size followed.

38.2% sales increase with slow-tempo background music vs. fast-tempo.

Milliman, R.E. (1982), Journal of Marketing

That was 1982 and it’s been replicated and extended many times since. The mechanism is simple enough that it should bother every retailer who hasn’t thought about it: the music playing in your store is either keeping customers in the environment longer, exposing them to more product, or moving them toward the exit before they’ve finished shopping.

Most stores have no spec for this

Here’s what I find genuinely strange. Retailers spend real money on store layout, fixture placement, lighting temperature, signage hierarchy, even scent. These are all environmental variables designed to affect customer behavior. And then the music, which is arguably the most persistent sensory input in the entire space, gets handed to a playlist algorithm or a store manager’s Spotify account.

The music is the most persistent sensory input in the retail environment. And most retailers treat it like elevator fill.

Nobody at Target sat down and said, “We want customers to move through this store at a pace consistent with 127 BPM.” Somebody picked a song because it sounded good. Maybe it charted recently. Maybe it tested well in some brand-alignment survey. The tempo was never part of the conversation, and the behavioral consequence was never measured.

This is the gap. Retail operators who want to increase dwell time, grow basket size, and improve revenue per visit are controlling every environmental variable except the one that runs continuously from open to close. The music is always on. It’s always affecting the customer’s pace and purchase behavior. The question is whether anyone specified it for the right outcome.

What the right tempo range actually looks like

If your goal is to get people to stay longer in a retail environment, the research consistently points to a tempo range of roughly 72 to 96 BPM for most store formats. A resting-to-walking human pace. Comfortable. It matches the speed at which people naturally browse when they’re not being pushed.

Above 100 BPM, you start to see measurable increases in walking speed and decreases in time spent in the store. By 120 and above, dwell time is already contracting. The customer might not consciously register that the music is fast, but their body already did.

Tempo is one variable in a larger set of flow factors that collectively shape the customer’s in-store experience. Mode matters too (major versus minor key), and so does rhythmic density, arrangement energy, and lyrical content. But tempo is the most measurable, the most studied, and the easiest to get wrong when nobody is paying attention.

Dwell time as a revenue variable

Dwell time correlates directly with basket size. A customer who spends 22 minutes in your store instead of 14 is touching more product, considering more add-ons, noticing the endcap they’d have walked past.

Every minute of additional dwell time is a chance for another item in the basket. The environmental variables that increase it (lighting, layout, scent, temperature, music) are force multipliers on the merchandise and staff you’ve already paid for. You don’t need more traffic to grow revenue per visit. You need the traffic you have to stay longer and buy more.

The inverse is also true. A store running high-tempo background music during low-traffic hours, like the Target I was standing in, is effectively shortening the time each customer spends on the floor. Fewer items considered. Smaller basket. Lower revenue per visit. And nobody in the building knows it’s happening because nobody specified the music for a behavioral outcome.

What a retailer can actually do about this

The first step is knowing what’s playing. That sounds obvious, but most store operators couldn’t tell you the average tempo of the music running in their space right now. Download a tempo-tracking app on your phone. Walk your floor during different dayparts. Tap along for 30 seconds. You’ll probably be surprised.

If your store is running above 100 BPM during hours when you want customers to linger and browse, you’re leaving money on the table. The music is a controllable variable. It runs all day. And unlike a fixture redesign or a staffing change, adjusting it costs almost nothing.

The harder version is specifying music at the parameter level. Designing the audio environment around a target tempo range, a target energy contour, a target emotional mode, all calibrated to the customer you’re trying to serve and the outcome you’re trying to produce. But even before you get to that conversation, the action item is simple. Go stand on your floor. Listen. Count the tempo. If it’s pushing 127, you now know something about your store that you probably didn’t know this morning. And you know what it’s costing you.
