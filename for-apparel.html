Forty years of evidence

Forty years of evidence on the levers that matter for high-traffic apparel.

Tempo affects pace and basket.

Milliman (1982) measured slower music increasing dwell. Knoferle et al. (2012) showed the effect depends on tempo paired with the right tonal choice — most catalogue vendors have the thermostat without the room.

Music customers read as theirs moves what they spend.

Areni and Kim (1993) found that in a wine cellar, customers chose more expensive bottles — not more of them — when the music matched the customer profile. North, Shilcock and Hargreaves (2003) found the same prestige-fit effect lifted spend per head in a restaurant. Same items on the rack, different customer read.

Familiar songs cut perceived dwell.

Yalch and Spangenberg (2000) found that familiar music made shoppers leave sooner. They reported enjoying it more. They left faster. Catalogue vendors play familiar songs because complaints are minimized — that is the wrong objective for conversion.

Mismatched music forfeits the lift.

Music that fits the customer category lifts what shoppers will pay; music that clashes gives that lift back (Areni and Kim 1993; North, Shilcock and Hargreaves 2003). Neutral is not the baseline. The audio environment is doing work in the room whether you chose its effect or not.
